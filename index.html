Jane
Goodall

I chose Jane Goodall for this project because she spent her whole life studying one small group of chimpanzees, and that patience ended up changing how scientists think about animals.

Born 3 April 1934 • London, England • Studied chimps at Gombe Stream, Tanzania
01 Full Name & Background

Her full name is Dame Jane Morris Goodall. She was born in London, England, on 3 April 1934, so she's British.

Even as a kid she loved animals. One story that gets told about her a lot: she once hid in a henhouse for hours, completely still, just to watch a hen lay an egg. That kind of patience is basically her whole career in one story.

What surprised me most researching this is that she didn't have a university science degree when she started her fieldwork. She learned by watching closely and writing everything down, not by studying it in a classroom first.

02 What Inspired Her

Two things pulled her toward Africa. As a child she had a stuffed toy chimpanzee named Jubilee, and she grew up reading Doctor Dolittle and Tarzan of the Apes over and over.

At 23, she saved up money from working as a waitress and sailed to Kenya. There she met a paleontologist named Louis Leakey. He believed that studying living great apes could tell us something about how early humans behaved — and he picked Jane specifically because she was patient and didn't come in with a lot of pre-formed scientific opinions. In 1960 he sent her to Gombe Stream in Tanzania to start observing wild chimpanzees, and that's really where her whole career begins.

03 What She Worked to Protect

Her main focus has always been wild chimpanzees and the forest they live in, but her work grew a lot bigger than that over time.

  • Chimpanzees — how they behave, their family bonds, and just keeping them alive in the wild.
  • The forest at Gombe — which was (and still is) threatened by logging and land being cleared for farms.
  • The people who live near the forest — her programs try to improve their lives too, since conservation doesn't really work if it ignores the communities around it.
  • Bigger environmental issues — habitat loss, animal welfare, and later on, climate change, as she became more of a global advocate and less just a field researcher.
04 Her Most Important Contribution

In 1960, she watched a chimpanzee she'd named David Greybeard strip the leaves off a twig and use it to pull termites out of a mound. Then he made another tool the exact same way.

This mattered a lot because up until then, scientists thought making tools was something only humans did. Her discovery basically forced people to rethink where the line is between humans and other animals. It also convinced Leakey to get her into Cambridge for a PhD, even though she'd never finished an undergraduate degree — which was almost unheard of at the time.

Later she founded the Jane Goodall Institute in 1977 and Roots & Shoots in 1991, which is a youth program that's now active in dozens of countries. She also became a United Nations Messenger of Peace.

06 What I Learned From Her
1

Being patient and just watching carefully can teach you things that rushing to a conclusion never will.

2

You don't need an official title before you start — she began with curiosity and a notebook, not a degree.

3

Protecting an animal usually means you also have to think about the people who live near it.

4

One person paying close attention to one small place — a single forest, one group of chimps — can end up changing how the whole world thinks.
