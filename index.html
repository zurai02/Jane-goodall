01 Who She Is

Dame Jane Morris Goodall was born in London, England, on 3 April 1934. She grew up fascinated by animals — as a girl, she once hid in a henhouse for hours just to watch a hen lay an egg.

She had no university science degree when she began her fieldwork. What she had instead was patience, careful eyes, and a notebook — the tools that would define her entire career.

02 What Sparked It

Two things pulled young Jane toward Africa: a stuffed toy chimpanzee named Jubilee, given to her as a child, and a battered copy of Doctor Dolittle and Tarzan of the Apes.

At 23, she saved enough money working as a waitress to sail to Kenya, where she met the paleontologist Louis Leakey. Leakey believed that studying living great apes could reveal clues about early human behavior — and he chose Jane, with her patience and lack of preconceived scientific bias, to study wild chimpanzees. In 1960, he sent her to Gombe Stream in Tanzania.

03 What She Worked to Protect

Goodall's central focus has always been wild chimpanzees and the forest habitat they depend on.

  • Chimpanzees — their behavior, family bonds, and survival in the wild.
  • Gombe's forest habitat — threatened by logging and land cleared for farming.
  • Communities around the forest — through programs that link human wellbeing to conservation.
  • Wider environmental issues — habitat loss, animal welfare, and climate change, as her work grew into global advocacy.
04 Her Biggest Contribution

In 1960, Goodall observed a chimpanzee she named David Greybeard strip leaves from a twig and use it to fish termites from a mound — then made another tool the same way.

Until that moment, toolmaking was considered a defining trait of humans alone. Her discovery forced scientists to rethink the line between humans and other animals, and it convinced Leakey to secure her a place at Cambridge to earn a PhD — without ever having completed an undergraduate degree first.

She went on to found the Jane Goodall Institute (1977) and Roots & Shoots (1991), a youth program now active in dozens of countries, and became a United Nations Messenger of Peace.

06 What We Can Learn
I

Patience and quiet observation can reveal what loud, quick conclusions miss.

II

You don't need a formal title to start — curiosity and careful notes are where real discovery begins.

III

Protecting an animal often means protecting the people and land around it too.

IV

One person's close attention to one small place — a single forest, a single group of chimps — can change how the whole world thinks.
